# Schools of Formalism

## Scope

This curriculum is designed to give individuals a broad, yet precise understanding of the foundations of Mathematical Formalisms. Put another way, it's designed to make individuals more comfortable with the language of math and it's various dialects across topics like:

- Physics
- Information theory
- Algorithms
- Set theory
- Predicate calculus
- Graph theory
- Linear Algebra
- Multivariate calculus
- Differential geometry
- Complex analysis
- Probability theory and statistics
- Discrete mathematics
- Abstract algebra
- Topology
- Functional analysis
- Quantum computing

This is not meant to be a deep dive into any one form of math. Rather, it's a primer on notation to make it easier to dive deeper into each of the subjects mentioned above. If you find yourself looking at equations and wondering what the variables and operations are, or if you're unsure why certain common operations are used in certain fields of math, this course is designed to help build that initial knowledge and intuition. It's a bridge from where you are now to being able to intelligently study and understand fields of math you're not already familiar with.

## Course Structure

The structure of this course will be comprised of a series of discussions on each covered mathematical dialect. Each lesson follows a consistent format:

### Core Notation
For each mathematical concept, we provide:
- **Conceptual explanation** - What the operation or concept actually means mathematically
- **Notation variations** - The different ways it's written across mathematical contexts
- **Usage examples** - Practical examples showing how it appears in real mathematical work
- **Context-specific details** - When and why certain notations are preferred in different fields

### Navigation Tools
- **Recognizing this field** - The key pieces of notation that immediately identify when you're looking at this particular field of mathematics
- **Translation tables** - How concepts and notation map between this field and related mathematical areas
- **Most likely encounters** - What academic or professional contexts you're most likely to encounter this notation in

## Foundational Discussion
Prior to this main body of discourse, we'll discuss the topic of formalism itself. What is it? How and why is it used? And most importantly, how to recognize good formalism and bad formalism–that's right, sometimes a provided equation really is just unnecessarily confusing or under-explained

## Why Work Through this Course?

I’m a software developer. Software developers are expected to be able to not just pick up on concepts quickly, but be able to pick them up across a multiplicity of languages, frameworks, tool sets, and individual repositories. I’ve found that the hardest parts of learning any language are A.) mapping existing concepts to “the way we do it around here” for the particular language, and B.) figuring out what’s actually new or different conceptually. Once that’s understood, reasoning in the language and adding to the code using the language becomes much simpler.

Math is just another programming language–and one with a number of disparate frameworks.

## Using this Course as Reference

If you are largely familiar with mathematical formalism already and just need a reference guide, this course is structured to support that. Each field lesson includes quick identification tools and cross-references to help you rapidly navigate between mathematical contexts. For notation conflicts and disambiguation, see the dedicated reference section which centralizes symbol conflicts across all mathematical fields.
